Lecture Notes: Human Intelligence vs. Artificial Intelligence

Here are the notes from the second lecture, focusing on Human Intelligence and its comparison to Artificial Intelligence: Introduction to Human Intelligence Understanding human intelligence helps us reflect on how AI differs from it and reveals AI’s fundamental limitations. Exploring human intelligence raises the question of whether or how AI should attempt to mimic it. Human intelligence spans from high-level cognitive theories down to low-level neurobiological building blocks. Artificial intelligence currently operates differently, relying heavily on data-driven pattern recognition. Brain Anatomy and Function The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain, divided into left and right hemispheres, and is responsible for high-level functions like reasoning, learning, and emotion. The cerebellum sits below the cerebrum and coordinates muscle movements, posture, and balance. The brainstem acts as a relay center connecting the brain to the spinal cord while managing automatic functions like heart rate and breathing. The frontal lobe of the cerebrum handles reasoning, motor control, emotion, and language. The temporal lobe enables conscious and long-term memory. The parietal lobe processes non-visual sensory information such as touch, taste, and temperature. The occipital lobe is dedicated to processing visual information. The cerebral cortex is the folded surface of the cerebrum, consisting of ridges called gyri and valleys called sulci. The outer cortex is made of “grey matter” (neurons), while the underlying long nerve fibers form “white matter” that connects different brain areas. The Parieto-Frontal Integration Theory suggests that higher intelligence stems from faster, more efficient, and selective information flow between the frontal and parietal regions, rather than just raw brain power. Neurobiology: The Building Blocks The cerebral cortex mostly consists of a 6-layered neocortex, with each layer containing different neuronal shapes, sizes, and densities. Neurons are the basic working units of the brain, featuring a cell body, dendrites for receiving information, and an axon for transmitting outputs. The human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons, each connecting to 1,000 to 10,000 other neurons, capable of storing around 1,000 terabytes of data. A synapse is the gap between neurons where signal transmission occurs. Electrical synapses provide direct current flow for almost instantaneous, highly reliable transmission without signal transformation. Chemical synapses are slower but offer flexibility by amplifying or inhibiting signals, which allows for changes in strength over time (the basis of learning). The human brain consumes about 20% of the body’s energy but operates on merely 20 watts of power. While brain signals travel at just 1 to 120 meters per second—much slower than computer processors—the brain still outperforms computers in many tasks. Cognitive Theories of Intelligence Jeff Hawkins’ “Thousand Brains Theory” proposes that all cortical columns learn models of objects, and connections between them resolve ambiguity through sensor fusion. The human brain functions using an “Old Brain” (subcortical regions responsible for basic survival functions) and a “New Brain” (the neocortex responsible for abstract thought and logic). Daniel Kahneman proposes two modes of human thought: System 1 (fast, automatic, emotional, unconscious) and System 2 (slow, effortful, logical, conscious). Minsky & Papert’s “Society of Minds” theory argues that intelligence is not a singular capability but a network of many small, specialized, non-intelligent agents working hierarchically. Multi-Faceted Intelligence Theories Theory Description Gardner’s Multiple Intelligences Differentiates intelligence into distinct modalities, such as logical-mathematical, linguistic, and spatial intelligence. Sternberg’s Triarchic Theory Divides intelligence into analytic (reasoning), creative (inventing), and practical (adapting to context) components. PASS Theory Focuses on Planning, Attention, Simultaneous, and Successive processing. Cattell-Horn-Carroll Theory Proposes a hierarchy consisting of general intelligence at the top, broad abilities (like fluid and crystallized intelligence) in the middle, and narrow, specific skills at the bottom. Piaget’s Stages of Cognitive Development Stage Age Range Characteristics Sensorimotor Birth to 2 years Gains knowledge of the world through physical interaction involving vision and motor skills. Pre-operational 2 to 7 years Uses symbols to represent objects and develops intuitive thoughts (asking why and how). Concrete operational 7 to 11 years Uses appropriate logic and begins to understand the perspectives of others. Formal operational 11 to 15 years Capable of abstract, hypothetical, and deductive reasoning to solve complex problems. Modern AI vs. Human Intelligence Modern AI functions mainly by finding patterns in training data associated with nominal labels or numeric values. AI heavily relies on its training data; it can be deceiving if it represents some cases correctly while missing others entirely. A prominent failure of this data reliance was Google Photos’ facial recognition model mislabeling African American individuals as gorillas due to biased training data. AI pattern recognition can still be highly valuable, akin to how John Snow historically identified the source of London’s cholera outbreak by finding geographical patterns. Current conversational AI agents are exploring “inner thoughts” to simulate System 2 thinking, utilizing intrinsic motivation to proactively engage users rather than just reacting. Expert Systems Expert systems were heavily developed before the second AI winter to make AI think more like humans. These systems derive rules directly from human experts to imitate reasoning, rather than relying on massive training datasets. IBM’s Deep Blue, which defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997, represents a peak achievement for expert systems. MYCIN: A 1970s medical expert system that used rules to select antibiotics for severe infections and could explain to users why it asked specific questions. DXplain: A 1980s clinical decision support system that acted as a medical reference, producing ranked diagnoses and justifications based on entered symptoms. In medical diagnosis, human doctors excel at the “generative” phase (broadly gathering information and forming hypotheses), while AI excels at the “discriminative” phase (narrowing down candidates based on laws and probabilities). The Gap Between AI and Consciousness AI successfully replicates some low-level biological primitives, such as using artificial neurons, layers, and weighted activations. We still lack a complete, validated theory of how true intelligence emerges from neurons. Modern AI sidesteps the unresolved question of true intelligence by simply relying on vast amounts of training data to parameterize low-level constructs. While intelligent machines can process information millions of times faster than biological brains, they cannot acquire entirely new knowledge at that same accelerated rate. Consciousness remains a major unsolved problem, specifically how physical brain processes create subjective experiences. From an evolutionary perspective, consciousness may have developed to make humans value existence and better anticipate/prepare for future events.
